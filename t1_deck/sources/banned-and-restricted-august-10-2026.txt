Standard
Written by Jadine Klomparens

For the last several months, Standard has been headlined by two strategies that are clearly ahead of the pack: 
Badgermole Cub
-based strategies in green and various decks utilizing blue-red noncreature spells. For most of this time, the gap between these two strategies and the rest of the format was discernable but not insurmountable, and many decks could find success each weekend.
The Standard metagame was diverse and changed over time. The green decks evolved from 
Ouroboroid
 decks to 
Nature's Rhythm
 decks to landfall strategies. The blue-red decks splintered into Izzet Prowess, Izzet Lessons, and Izzet Spellementals. We saw new decks like Mardu Discard and Four-Color Control emerge. During this period, Standard had some problems, but it was healthy overall, and we elected to not ban cards during our updates to the banned and restricted list.
Recently, the gap between the top decks and the rest of the field has widened. The biggest development is a new 
Badgermole Cub
 deck called Selesnya Offense that has emerged as the strongest green variant. Like previous variants that incorporated 
Nature's Rhythm
, its focus is on ending the game as quickly as possible with the help of 
Practiced Offense
 from 
Secrets of Strixhaven
. This iteration is more resilient and difficult to attack thanks to its utility lands and a creature suite focused on resilience, including a hexproof threat in 
Leatherhead, Swamp Stalker
.

The world of competitive 
Magic
 has largely shifted its attention to Modern over the last two months with the Modern Regional Championship Qualifier (RCQ) season and Pro Tour 
Magic: The Gathering
® | 
Marvel Super Heroes
 receiving the brunt of competitive pressure. Because of this, there haven't been high-level tournaments for Selesnya Offense to succeed in, and our picture of the shape of the Standard metagame is fuzzier than it would be if Standard were under a higher degree of competitive pressure.
Despite this lack of in-person tournaments to look at, the story told by our data from 
MTG Arena
 is clear. Selesnya Offense has the highest win rate by a large margin, winning over 57% of its matches, and it also has the second-highest play rate. Normally, win rates fall as play rates climb. Decks that can maintain high win rates while also having high play rates are large threats to the balance of formats. In fact, the overall profile of the Selesnya Offense deck in our data is very similar to what 
Vivi Ornitier
's and 
Agatha's Soul Cauldron
's data looked like at the time that we banned Vivi Ornitier.
Most of the time, when a new deck breaks onto the scene with a high win rate and quickly picks up steam, it's not cause for alarm. It takes time for players to figure out how to play against the new strategy and adapt their sideboards and strategies to face it. In this case, Selesnya Offense is not a brand-new deck. It's an evolution of one of the format's pillars and represents a new balance of resiliency and speed with few weaknesses. The combination of a sustained high win rate and play rate in our data in addition to 
Badgermole Cub
's history of dominance is concerning.
Rather than wait for the upcoming Standard RCQ season to prove that Selesnya Offense will be problematic for in-person Standard, we're electing to act now. Banning cards in Standard is something we take very seriously. We generally err toward being conservative with changes in Standard. Standard being stable is important for its long-term health. The risk that this Standard format becomes severely imbalanced and not fun to play is high enough for us to take action today. Our goal with today's changes is to pave the way for Standard to be a fun and diverse format as competitive pressure returns to it.
Badgermole Cub is the clear choice for a card to ban from Selesnya Offense. 
Badgermole Cub
 has been an extremely powerful and format-defining card in Standard ever since it was released. We've discussed it at length in several previous banned and restricted announcements. It is a cheap threat that demands an immediate answer and lets its controller develop an insurmountable mana advantage. The pressure 
Badgermole Cub
 exerts on decks to be able to answer a two-mana creature forces them to value mana efficiency highly and compresses the format in unhealthy ways. Without 
Badgermole Cub
, Standard will be more fun and have more breathing room.
Our data shows that Standard has three distinct decks at the top in terms of metagame share. We've discussed the top deck (in terms of win rate) at length already: Selesnya Offense. The other two most played decks are Jeskai Lessons and Izzet Prowess, which represent the blue-red side of this Standard environment's historic dichotomy. To prevent one of these established decks from taking over as the best deck in the format after the banning of 
Badgermole Cub
, we are electing to also ban a card from Izzet Prowess and a card from Jeskai Lessons.

Izzet Prowess has been strongest among the various blue-red variants for a while. It is a fast and aggressive deck capable of winning the game early while also maintaining a high degree of interaction. Watching Izzet Prowess during this Standard environment has taught us that we need to be more deliberate with Prowess and cards that reward cheap spells in a three-year Standard schedule. A larger card pool leads to greater access to cheap spells. 
Stormchaser's Talent
 is Izzet Prowess's keystone card, and the card we are electing to ban.
Stormchaser's Talent
 is a one-mana card that lets Izzet Prowess pull off to fast starts and gives the deck a strong late game thanks to its interaction with 
Boomerang Basics
. This combination of early-game and late-game strength is something we think is inappropriate for a one-mana card. Cards that cheap shouldn't be so well-rounded and shouldn't compete with more expensive cards as the game goes long. Without 
Stormchaser's Talent
, blue-red decks will be a little slower and easier to overcome in the late game.

Lessons decks are a blue-red variant that have been consistently strong in Standard since release. Jeskai Lessons is a new evolution of them that incorporates 
Tablet of Discovery
 and 
Jeskai Revelation
 to gain a powerful late game. It's one of the variations of blue-red that doesn't commonly play 
Stormchaser's Talent
, and it has the highest play rate in Standard on 
MTG Arena
. To prevent Lessons variants from taking the top slot in the next iteration of Standard, we're choosing to ban 
Gran-Gran
.
Gran-Gran
 is a powerful mana engine for Lessons decks. Like 
Badgermole Cub
, it demands a quick answer due to its snowbally nature. Like 
Stormchaser's Talent
, it's an inappropriately threatening one-mana card that provides too much late-game advantage for a card that costs one mana. 
Gran-Gran
 is in a unique spot as Jeskai Lessons is light on creatures, making counterplay against the deck more difficult. It demands an answer, but creature removal is weak against the rest of the Jeskai Lessons deck.
With all three of today's bans, we are acting to improve the metagame diversity of Standard and slow down the format. The biggest gameplay issue of this Standard environment has been its speed. The strongest cards and strategies have forced decks to interact earlier to compete in the metagame, putting immense pressure on decks to compress their mana curves as much as possible. 
Badgermole Cub
 decks have historically been capable of winning games as early as turn three, and they commonly win on turn four or five. Izzet Prowess is similarly capable of ending games in the early turns. This is faster than we want Standard to be.
Magic
 Design has learned much from this particular Standard environment. We have greatly increased our level of scrutiny on one- and two-mana cards that demand an answer to prevent early snowbally starts. Similarly, we've put a lot of thought and energy into what we want the speed of Standard to be and how we can design cards to make that a reality. These adjustments have been in the works for a while, and we will soon start to see sets release where those learnings are incorporated.

Slumbering Trudge
 and 
Fight Rigging

Magic Online

The previously mentioned Neoform, however, is a deck we're paying very close attention to.

Neoform has fallen in and out of favor in Modern, which is something that we like to see. It can be concerning, however, when we see the number of weekends that are favorable for it increase over time. The deck uses 
Allosaurus Rider
 to put a creature with a high mana value onto the battlefield before sacrificing it with 
Neoform
 or 
Eldritch Evolution
 to cheat another big creature into play. From there, it tends to win with other forms of cheating the mana system.
This type of strategy, which manipulates the rules engine of 
Magic
 is something that we think is cool to exist at a baseline, and we like to see innovative deck builders try to push these decks into the competitive scene. Dredge and Lantern Control are similar decks that have become iconic for the way that they use cards in some of the most mind-bending applications imaginable. Neoform poses a problem on top of its novelty: game compression. Because it's so all or nothing and has the ability to win on turn two, we have concerns that the deck could make Modern worse if it becomes a regular player, seeing ten percent or more of the metagame share in any given Modern tournament.
That being said, we don't think it's anywhere near that state now.
Overall, Modern looks like a place where any given deck is exploitable and can be beaten, and we're happy to see that degree of self-correction in our nonrotating formats. We appreciate the degree to which Standard-appropriate cards have found homes in Modern over the last couple of years, and we look forward to seeing what comes with 
Magic: The Gathering
® | 
The Hobbit
™.

Legacy
Written by Carmen Klomparens
The Fantasticar
 is banned.
Since the release of 
Magic: The Gathering
 | 
Marvel Super Heroes
, we've seen a bunch of different cards make their way into nonrotating 
Magic
 formats, with one card standing above the rest in Legacy and Vintage: 
The Fantasticar
.
When 
The Fantasticar
 was first previewed, it didn't take long for it to catch the community's eye. It has a low cost and is extremely explosive, playing well with many of the tools that already define Eternal formats. Despite this early attention, Studio X is quite philosophically reticent to take preemptive action against cards in Eternal formats. The tools we use to answer specific strategies are extensive. For a lot of players, the high power level of these formats is their appeal.
However, 
The Fantasticar
 has proven to be near impossible to address with any single series of cards because of how many different shells it appears in.

The fact that

The Fantasticar
. We've seen it in over a dozen different archetypes and are extremely concerned about that level of ubiquity.
While it isn't necessarily a problem for cards in supplemental products to show up in Legacy, this level of impact is significantly out of bounds for cards from any type of release. We're constantly improving how we make 
Magic

Magic
 formats. We're optimistic that the fresh heuristics we've begun to apply in the time since the development cycle for 
The Fantasticar
 will catch future designs that carry the degree of risk this incurred in Eternal formats.

 together. For both ubiquity and power-level reasons, we are acting against 
The Fantasticar
 now rather than later. We're looking forward to getting a snapshot of Legacy post-
Candelabra of Tawnos
 ban that doesn't have the pressure of 
The Fantasticar
 compressing games across the board.

Brawl
Written by Dave Finseth
No changes
Back in June, we took significant action by banning six cards in Brawl. We took a stricter stance on efficient mana acceleration, low-cost countermagic, and easily looped cards that grant extra turns. Player feedback on these changes was overwhelmingly positive, and the data shows a promising, healthier future for Brawl.
We are not making any additional changes today as we think it's healthy to give players some additional time to explore the format. We have been reviewing and discussing other individual cards internally, but nothing has emerged as an outlier in terms of win rate or play rate.
As far as matchmaking goes, our last round of changes has made queue times slightly longer by about four seconds on average, but this has increased the percentage of matches against commanders of a similar power level. We think this is a reasonable trade-off but will continue to monitor matchmaking across several metrics to keep improving how we connect players for fun games of 
Magic
.

Competitive Brawl
Written by Dave Finseth
No changes
We are delighted by how balanced Competitive Brawl has become in the months since its release. Currently, no individual commander makes up more than one percent of the metagame, and win rates for the most competitive decks are within bounds of each other. Additionally, we see many players exploring new commander options from sets soon after they release. Competitive Brawl is ripe for exploration and development! We are excited to see how the metagame establishes itself and will certainly take action if it becomes necessary.
